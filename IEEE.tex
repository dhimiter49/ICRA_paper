\begin{abstract}
    Current robot navigation research focuses on safety and collaborative behaviour. While deep learning achieves remarkable results, it neglects safety guarantees. We employ passive safety via model predictive control (MPC) to ensure at least some level of constraint. However, this introduces other challenges, e.g. freezing robot problem (FRP) and in general worse collaboration with the crowd. Collaboration is an all-encompassing term closely related to human robot interaction (HRI). In navigation, collaborative behaviour should be comfortable, legible, polite, abiding to social norms, agent understanding, proactive and context appropriate behaviour \cite{francis_principles_2025}. We adapt to the MPC-objective a learned path through Reinforcement Learning (RL), serving to better take into consideration the dense and dynamic crowd. The objective of our work is to explore a framework that ensures safety guarantees while integrating desirable collaborative navigation. We argue that it is possible to achieve this by combining classical, constraint-adherent methods as MPC to learning approaches in order to efficiently be safe in social scenarios. We evaluate the effects this hybrid approach has on efficiency, safety and FRP while comparing to SOTA algorithms.

\end{abstract}


\section{INTRODUCTION}\label{sec:intro}
    Robot navigation is gaining traction as newer technologies enable robots to coexist with humans. In \cite{fraichard_short_2007} three criteria are proposed to address the task, a robot should consider its dynamics, the future behaviour of the objects in the environment and do this over a long enough time-horizon. While robot dynamics and horizon settings are manageable, predicting the future is far from trivial which makes the problem NP-hard (formalized for motion planning \cite{demaine_a_general}). In case of a perfect prediction, a path finding algorithm would suffice. Instead the common approaches nowadays are: geometric methods i.e. dynamic window approach (DWA) \cite{fox_dynamic_1997} or inevitable collision states \cite{fraichard_inevitable_2003}, model predictive control (MPC) \cite{schouwenaars_receding_2004,bohorquez_safe_2016,ciocca_balance_2020} and finally learning approaches \cite{alahi_2016_CVPR,everett_collision_2021,liu_intention_2023,moder_navigating_2024}. Research focuses on the latter two as DWA presented limitations \cite{rhino}.

    % omitted literature solovey_complexity, patel_dwa-rl_2021, dorante_design_2018, minerva, han_deep_2022

    MPC optimizes its objective by modeling dynamics and using a fixed horizon in a feedback control loop with linear constraints for convex objectives. Real-time control is achievable using standard hardware and fast QP-solvers \cite{goulart_clarabel_2024}. Through constraints it is possible to ensure  \textit{passive safety} \cite{macek_towards_2009} which allows only collisions where the robot is static. Nevertheless, MPC can be overly restrictive, leading to the freezing robot problem (FRP) \cite{trautman_unfreezing_2010}. To alleviate this, a perfect model of the future is not enough. In dense crowds, it is necessary to implement joint-collision-avoidance, so that the robot takes action under the assumption that other members of the crowd also try to avoid collisions.

    % omitted literature osqp, bambade2025tro

    We address the problem by learning trajectories that comply with the state and future intent of the crowd. This has the same effect as \cite{sathyamoorthy_frozone_2020} of avoiding potential states where FRP may happen, conversely we do not explicitly define control mechanics to evade freezing zones. We hypothesize that through joint learning of RL with an MPC controller it is feasible to combine the strengths of both approaches. On the one hand we learn efficient, amenable trajectories thanks to RL and on the other hand it is possible to guarantee passive safety. Moreover, using an intrinsic reward, the RL policy will better learn to adapt to the safety maneuvers of MPC.

    Our work is inspired by the current attempt to unify MPC and RL \cite{reiter_synthesis_2025}. We make use of the same theory as \cite{tearle_predictive_2021,wabersich_predictive_2022,kicki_bridging_2024} on safety filters for learning. Our key difference is, learning a trajectory for the robot to follow, which better incorporates infinite-horizon information from episodic RL. Complementary, MPC serves as a safety check over the RL policy that can make small mistakes in its prediction.

    % omitted literature recht_tour_2018, brito_where_2021, strawn_conformal_2023

    Our main contribution is a novel method to combine classic robot navigation with learning and analyze its advantages and limitations. We improve performance in key metrics like \textit{success rate} and \textit{collision rate} when enhancing MPC with RL while being bound by the explosion of constraints in dense scenarios. This implies a trade-off between collaboration through joint-collision-avoidance and theoretical safety. Full adherence to social principles \cite{francis_principles_2025}, is beyond the scope of this paper but remains the focus for future work.

\section{PROBLEM FORMULATION}\label{sec:problem}

The navigation problem can be formulated as a \textit{\acrlong*{mdp}} (\acrshort*{mdp}) defined by the tuple $(\mathcal{S,A,P,R})$, where $\mathcal{S}$, $\mathcal{A}$ are the \textit{state space} and \textit{action space} respectively, $\mathcal{P}(s,a,s')$ is the \textit{transition probability} from $s\in\mathcal{S}$ to $s'\in\mathcal{S}$ via $a\in\mathcal{A}$ and $\mathcal{R}:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ is the \textit{reward function}. The way the components of the \acrshort*{mdp} are defined, describe a specific task. In navigation, the task involves an agent moving through a crowd with a given goal position $\boldsymbol{p}_g$. Additionally to $\boldsymbol{p}_g$, the state is described by the position, velocity, shape and orientation of each obstacle and the agent itself. In this paper we use the term \textit{agent} and \textit{robot} interchangeably and the term \textit{crowd} to refer to people in the environment.

\subsection{Reinforcement Learning}

RL draws inspiration from the natural learning process of positive and negative reinforcement, where animals try to increase positive stimulus and decrease negative ones based on biological needs. I.e., avoiding climbing trees after a bad fall and search for more blueberries to satisfy hunger. Artificially, a feedback signal ($\mathcal{R}$) mimics the stimulus. An environment is necessary to train the agent through trial and error by gathering data as opposed to supervised learning where an existing dataset is needed. RL has been shown to surpass humans in various task, most commonly the Atari 2600 games are used as benchmark \cite{mnih_human-level_2015}.

The objective in RL is to solve the MDP by maximizing the expected return over time at each time-step $t$
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:rl}
    G_t=\sum\limits^\infty_{k=0}\gamma^k\mathbb{E}_{(s_{t+k}, a_{t+k})\sim\rho} \mathcal{R}(s_{t+k}, a_{t+k}),
\end{equation}
where $\rho$ represents the state-actions marginal distribution and $\gamma$ ($0\leq\gamma < 1$) is a MDP parameter that limits $G_t$ for bounded $\mathcal{R}$. RL algorithms often use a value function (VF) $V(s)$ or $Q(s,a)$ (referred to also as critic) to evaluate the objective and steer learning. The VF estimates the expected reward over time starting from a given state or state-action pair. The most direct way to solve the problem and train an agent is policy gradient (PG). PG calculates a gradient towards the objective. The policy is implemented as a parameterized Gaussian distribution $\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(a\vert s)=\mathcal{N}(a\vert \mu_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(s), \Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(s))$ and it is also used to explore the environment. The objective can be written in terms of the parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ to be learned
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:policy_obj}
    G_t(\boldsymbol{\theta})&=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\tau)}\left[ \sum\limits^\infty_{k=0}\gamma^k \mathcal{R}(s_{t+k}, a_{t+k})\right] \text{with}, \\
    p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\tau)=p&(s_0)\prod\limits^\infty_{k=0}\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(a_{t+k}\vert s_{t+k})P(s_{t+k+1}\vert s_{t+k},a_{t+k}). \nonumber
\end{align}
Using Mont-Carlo estimate with $L$ samples and the log-ratio trick ($\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\log p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\tau})p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\tau})=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\tau})$) the gradient over the policy is computed from \Cref{eq:policy_obj}
% \begin{align}
%     \label{eq:policy_update}
%     \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} G(\boldsymbol{\theta})\approx \dfrac{1}{L}\sum\limits_i^L &\left(\sum\limits_{t} \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \log(\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(a_{i,t}\vert s_{i,t}))\right) \\
%     & \underbrace{\left(\sum\limits_{t}\gamma^t \mathcal{R}(s_{i,t}, a_{i,t})\right)}_{\text{approximate by} \; V^{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{old}}} \; \text{or} \; Q^{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{old}}}}. \nonumber
%     % \text{from} \; \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\log p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}&(\boldsymbol{\tau})=\dfrac{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\tau})}{p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\tau})} \; \text{ and \Cref{eq:policy_obj}}. \nonumber
% \end{align}

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:policy_update}
    \resizebox{.9\hsize}{!}{$
    \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} G(\boldsymbol{\theta})\approx \dfrac{1}{L}\sum\limits_i^L \left(\sum\limits_{t} \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \log\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(a_{i,t}\vert s_{i,t})\right) \underbrace{\left(\sum\limits_{t}\gamma^t \mathcal{R}(s_{i,t}, a_{i,t})\right)}_{\text{approx.by} \; V^{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{old}}} \; \text{or} \; Q^{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{old}}}}.
    $}
    % \text{from} \; \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\log p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}&(\boldsymbol{\tau})=\dfrac{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\tau})}{p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\tau})} \; \text{ and \Cref{eq:policy_obj}}. \nonumber
\end{equation}

\subsection{Model Predictive Control}

MPC can be used to solve control problems similar to RL. It needs a model of the system in order to predict its future behavior over a finite time. The application of MPC goes back 50 years and was originally used for complex industrial systems in chemical engineering to optimize operations and handle constraints. The theory was developed further starting from the 90' \cite{mayne_receding_1990} and now finds a wide application in industry, robotics and the automotive sector.

The optimization problem for MPC can be written as
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:mpc}
    \resizebox{.95\hsize}{!}{$
    \max \;\mathbb{E}\left[\sum\limits^N_{k=0}\mathcal{R}(s_{t+k}, a_{t+k})\right] \; \text{s.t.} \; s_{t+k+1} = f(s_{t+k}, a_{t+k}),
    $}
\end{equation}
where the cost minimization objective is replaced with reward maximization. The main differences between \Cref{eq:rl} and \Cref{eq:mpc} are: the infinite horizon used in RL opposed to a fixed MPC horizon and the model $f$ approximating $\mathcal{P}$ in MPC. In \Cref{eq:policy_update} the dynamics are unneeded to learn the policy. MPC optimizes control for a $N$-steps based on its model, however the model is imperfect thus requiring feedback-control at each step. The pseudo-optimal solution for the complete horizon is computed using a QP-solver.

\section{RELATED WORK}\label{sec:rel_work}

Research on robot navigation has converged to MPC and learning-based approaches. MPC has been used for whole body control in robotics as well as navigation \cite{wieber_viability_2008,ciocca_time_2023}. Recently, data-based approaches have gained popularity with the increase in real world data and sharp boost of compute in order to power deep learning algorithms. Human navigation dataset are available and are used to train prediction models, but in robot navigation, human trajectories do not indicate ground truths for robots. Predictive models \cite{alahi_2016_CVPR} can provide additional information for control to improve navigation performance. RL is the most common learn-based control method. Multiple works explore the combination of different architectures to improve the feature extraction of the relations in the crowd and refine planning capabilities \cite{chen_2017,chen_crowd-robot_2019,everett_collision_2021}.

Increasingly, both approaches are combined in order to enhance the performance of the MPC planner. In \cite{che_efficient_2020} a model approximates humans as optimal agents and an MPC planner uses the model to generate communicative actions. \cite{brito_where_2021} enhances MPC by learning subgoals with deep RL and \cite{xiao_motion_2022} uses a Transformer to learn the cost function for MPC. Other approaches combining MPC and RL address different or similar tasks. Work from \cite{hewing_learning-based_2020,tearle_predictive_2021,mesbah_fusion_2022} focuses on using MPC as a safety filter for actions predicted from RL agents. In this work we also concentrate on safety, however propose an episode-based RL approach and apply it to navigation in order to better fuse the advantages of MPC and RL. Our solution is close to the method proposed in \cite{kicki_bridging_2024} with the main difference concerning algorithmic choices.

We propose ProDMP \cite{li_prodmps_2022} for trajectory representation and TRPL \cite{otto_differentiable_2020} for learning to parametrize trajectories which have been shown to work well together \cite{otto_deep_2023}. ProDMP allows for smooth trajectories when replanning which is ideal in cases where the environment is unpredictable and feedback control is preferred. Moreover, it can learn trajectories with high sample efficiency when paired with TRPL. TRPL is a gradient-based, on-policy RL algorithm that proposes advantages compared to the widely used PPO algorithm in terms of theoretical consistency w.r.t. the trust-region (TR) formulation and robustness to implementation details.

\section{METHOD}

We use an episodic-RL approach and apply MPC to minimize the distance from the learned reference trajectory while ensuring hard constraints. The two methods are independent from one another and it is part of the objective of the RL agent to learn a behaviour that best takes advantage of MPC.

\subsection{MPC for Navigation of a Holonomic Robot}\label{sec:mpc}

We assume a holonomic robot implying dynamics from classical mechanics for agent position $\boldsymbol{p}_k$ at time $k$
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:dynamics}
    &\boldsymbol{p}_{k+1} = \boldsymbol{p}_k + \dot{\boldsymbol{p}}_kT + \frac{1}{2}T^2\ddot{\boldsymbol{p}}_k = \boldsymbol{p}_k + \frac{1}{2}T(\dot{\boldsymbol{p}}_k+\dot{\boldsymbol{p}}_{k+1})\\
    &\quad\: \text{and} \quad \dot{\boldsymbol{p}}_{k+1} = \dot{\boldsymbol{p}}_k+T\ddot{\boldsymbol{p}}_k \; \Leftrightarrow \; \ddot{\boldsymbol{p}}_k=\dfrac{\dot{\boldsymbol{p}}_{k+1}-\dot{\boldsymbol{p}}_k}{T}.\nonumber
\end{align}
To simplify the problem and reduce numerical complexity, velocity control is used. We further omit step indexes when possible to avoid clutter. Iteratively we derive the transition matrix for horizon $N$ and initial $\boldsymbol{p}_0$ based on \Cref{eq:dynamics}

            M_{xv} & \boldsymbol{0} \\
            \boldsymbol{0} & M_{yv} \\
        \end{bmatrix},
\end{align}

where $P^y$ is analogous to $P^y$, $M_{xv}=M_{yv}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}$. In the following, $x$ and $y$ will not be treated separately and we concatenate appropriately to get $P_0=\begin{bmatrix}

After modeling $P$, we define the cost function as the distance to a reference trajectory (e.g. straight line to goal), $\frac{1}{2}\vert\vert P - P^{ref}\vert\vert$. This gives us a quadratic function to optimize with \Cref{eq:dynamics_horizon}
\begin{align*}
    &\min\limits_{\dot{P}}\frac{1}{2}\vert\vert P - P^{ref}\vert\vert \Leftrightarrow \min\limits_{\dot{P}}\frac{1}{2}\left(P-P^{ref}\right)^\intercal\left(P-P^{ref}\right) \Leftrightarrow \nonumber\\
    &\min\limits_{\dot{P}} \frac{1}{2}\dot{P}^\intercal\underbrace{M_{pv}^\intercal M_{pv}}_{\substack{=Q \\ \text{quadratic term} }}\dot{P} +  \underbrace{\left(P_0-P^{ref}+ \frac{1}{2}T\dot{P}_0\right)^\intercal M_{pv}}_{=q \;\text{linear term}}\dot{P}.\nonumber
\end{align*}


Linear constraints are arbitrarily added to this objective, see \Cref{tab:constraints}. Passive safety is ensured by explicitly setting the last step of the control vector $\dot{P}$ to zero. Velocity and acceleration constraints are non-linear as we assume a threshold value in all directions. The circle representing the constraint is linearized using an octagon. Each edge of the octagon is represented by an inequality $y - m_ix-b_i<0$. This implies multiplying $x$ (the first half of $\dot{P}$) by $m_i$ while $y$ remains the same, and the constant $b_i$ is moved to the right side. In order to calculate the acceleration from the given velocity path, velocities at steps $k+1$ and $k$ are subtracted and divided by $T$, from \Cref{eq:dynamics} which is parallelized with matrix $D$. $\ddot{\boldsymbol{p}}_1$  depends on $\dot{\boldsymbol{p}}_0$, written on the right side of the inequality. Finally, the crowd constraint is linearized by assuming that the agent will follow the last computed MPC trajectory $\boldsymbol{\bar{p}}$. We also assume that the members of the crowd maintain the current velocity and increase the safety distance by the maximum distance in the error of the prediction. The maximum error will be lower than the distance covered during time-step $T$ with $\dot{\boldsymbol{p}}_{max}$, as represented in \Cref{fig:uncert}.

\subsection{Learning Trajectories for Navigation}\label{sec:prodmp}

Episode-based RL can be more advantageous then step-based RL in case of non-Markovian environments, environments where atomic steps are inaccessible or when learning temporal dependencies might be ineffective. Using motion primitives implies an episode representing a task. These approach to learning is motivated biologically as it has been well studied that certain movements are directly encoded in the nervous system. ML methods were proposed using the damped-spring equations to model motion primitives \cite{Schaal2006} and more recently extended \cite{paraschos_probabilistic_2013,li_prodmps_2022}. The conventional spring-model $-ky-c\dot{y}+f_{ext}=m\ddot{y}$ with spring constants $k$ and $c$, mass $m$ and external forces $f_{ext}$ is written as
% omitted literature ijspeert_dynamical_2013
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:spring_model}
    \tau^2\ddot{y}=\alpha(\beta(g-y)-\tau\dot{y})-f(x),
\end{equation}
where $g$ represents the goal attractor point, $\alpha,\beta$ are spring-damper constants, $f$ is the forcing term which we can define arbitrarily in order to execute desired trajectories and $\tau$ is used to control execution time. To cover the whole spectrum of desired movements, a weighted exponential basis functions scheme represents $f$, motivated in \cite[Section 2.1]{Schaal2006}
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:forcing_term}
    f(x)=x\dfrac{\sum_i\phi_i(x)w_i}{\sum_i\phi_i(x)}, \; \phi_i(x)=\exp{\left(\dfrac{-(x-\mu_i)^2}{2\sigma_i^2}\right)}.
\end{equation}
In this way the desired motion is conditioned by the weight parameters $w_i$ where $i$ indexes the basis dimension. In our case the number of basis function is set to $4$ while the dimension of the motion is in the $xy$ plane so 2D ($y\in\mathbb{R}^2$).

The advantage of using ProDMP \cite{li_prodmps_2022} is that it combines the strengths of DMPs (smooth replanning throughout the trajectory) and ProMPs (modelling of statistical properties) while requiring one offline basis function $\boldsymbol{\Phi}$ computation instead of costly online numerical integration of the dynamical system. The replanning step $r$ is set arbitrarily and allows for better control as newer information becomes available. The Gaussian policy is used to sample $\boldsymbol{w}\sim\mathcal{N}(\cdot\vert\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})$ to parameterize the forcing function giving a trajectory $\boldsymbol{\lambda}=y_{1:M}$ using \Cref{eq:spring_model} and \Cref{eq:forcing_term}.

The policy is trained with TRPL \cite{otto_differentiable_2020}, a PG-based method that implements differentiable TRs to avoid policy collapse by limiting updates for a distance metric $D$ and tunable $\epsilon$
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:tr_rl}
    \boldsymbol{\theta}^\star&=\argmax_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{(s,a)\sim p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\tau)}\left[Q^{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{old}}}(s,a)\right], \nonumber \\
    &\text{s.t.}\quad D(\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{old}}, \pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}) < \epsilon,\quad \forall s\in\mathcal{S}.
\end{align}
TRPL introduces two advantages compared to PPO \cite{schulman2017ppo}: stability w.r.t. code-level optimizations \cite{Engstrom2020Implementation} and the capacity to constrain states individually. This is achieved by applying projection layers to the policy output using a predefined distance to project the mean and variance. The objective in \Cref{eq:tr_rl} is simplified by using a Q-function, even though commonly a weighted IS advantage function is used. The usual actions $a$ in this case are the weights $\boldsymbol{w}$ to parameterize the ProDMP. The policy and VF share the same backbone architecture using 1D CNN for feature extraction of the LiDAR information and a fully-connected layer for the remaining observation. The full architecture and decision making pipeline for the next action, is shown in \Cref{fig:policy}.

\subsection{MPC as Safety Filter for Episode-based RL}\label{sec:const}

We propose to combine both approaches by setting the reference trajectory in the MPC objective to the prediction from the ProDMP TRPL policy $P^{ref}\coloneqq \boldsymbol{\lambda}$. It should hold that $M>N$ and an appropriate replanning rate $r$ is important. A high replanning period will results in worse predictions for steps far in the future. A short horizon will increase computational cost while avoiding jerky behaviour thanks to the properties of ProDMP. The reference trajectory is truncated for MPC and optimization is executed in a closed-loop control manner until replanning is reengaged. In Algorithm \ref{algo:main}, the two stages of the policy are shown. In lines 3-5 the new trajectory is computed, in line 6 the reference trajectory for the MPC objective is set. In lines 7 and 8 the necessary matrices, vectors are updated in order to run the QP optimization (line 9) with constraints.

While learning should facilitate a symbiotic relation between the two components, various considerations can accelerate learning. This entails hyperparameters like the \textit{stability coefficient} for MPC in order to encourage smooth control, the horizon $N$ for MPC and the \textit{goal scale} for ProDMP to use a suitable trajectory length. The stability term is an especially important factor when implementing the intrinsic reward
\begin{align*}
    r_i = \alpha\sum_{k=0}^{r}\left\vert\left\vert P_{t+k} - P^{ref}_{t+k}\right\vert\right\vert,
\end{align*}
as the MPC behaviour becomes unpredictable for RL without the term. There are limitations to the effect of the intrinsic component for highly jerky control from MPC. This can happen when considering uncertainty in the crowd prediction. In order to ensure passive safety, the crowd distance $d$ is augmented for future positions by the maximum error in prediction, shown in \Cref{fig:uncert}. With this conservative constraint the FRP becomes more noticeable. We use the QP-solver \cite{goulart_clarabel_2024} with tolerance parameters of $10^{-5}$, which takes on average $3ms$ to find a solution and a maximum of $11ms$, well under our time-step $T$ of $100ms$. Moreover, constraints are removed when possible in order to lower the computational footprint. As default values we replan $r$ every $2$ steps ($0.2s$) and set the MPC horizon $N$ to $20$ ($2s$). The batch size is $131$k and 10 backpropagation steps are executed per update iteration. On average TRPL with ProDMP converges after 30M environment steps on constant velocity crowd and around 100M steps when using ORCA.
\begin{algorithm}[t!]
\caption{Inference of ProDMP MPC}\label{algo:main}
    \KwData{$\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ ($\boldsymbol{\Phi}$, M, $r$), MPC ($N, M_{pv}, M_i, b_i, d$), $env$}
$s_t \gets env.init()$\;
\While{$t < env.max\_steps$}{
    \If{$t \mod r = 0$ }{
        $\boldsymbol{w}_t \gets \pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(s_t)$\;
        $\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t:t+M} \gets \boldsymbol{\Phi}\boldsymbol{w}_t$ \Comment*[r]{Eq. \ref{eq:spring_model}, \ref{eq:forcing_term}}
    }
    $P^{ref} \gets \boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t:t+N}$\;
    \tcp{Matrices \& crowd prediction}
    $\{Q, q, \hat{U}_c,P_c\} \gets mat(s_t,M_{pv},M_i,b_i,P^{ref},d) $\;
    \tcp{Constraints as in \Cref{tab:constraints}}
    $\boldsymbol{c_t} \gets const(s_t,Q,q,M_{pv},M_i,b_i,d,\hat{U}_c,P_c) $\;
    $\dot{P}^\star \gets \argmin_{\dot{P}}\vert\vert P - P^{ref}\vert\vert$ s.t. $c_{t,i} \:\forall i$\;
    $a_t \gets \dot{P}^\star_1$ \Comment*[r]{Feedback control}
    $s_t, r_t, done_t \gets env.step(a_t)$\;
}
\end{algorithm}


\section{EVALUATION}\label{sec:eval}

We evaluate our approach in three main perspectives: efficiency as a means to measure collaboration, safety and FRP. We also compare two aspects: the improvements brought to MPC and RL when combining the two and the difference to a baseline step-based RL algorithm SAC \cite{haarnojaZAL18} (better than PPO in our testing), an episode-based RL algorithm ProDMP with TRPL with velocity controller and the state of the art \cite{everett_collision_2021}. For the latter an evaluation benchmark is already implemented in \verb!gym-collision-avoidance! (GCA) \footnote{\url{link/to/gca_environment}}. The general setup involves multiple agents controlled by arbitrary policies with each a goal position. No static obstacles or walls are considered in this environment, however the observation would remain the same since we use LiDAR. We use \textit{return}, \textit{time to goal} (TTG) and \textit{success rate} (reaching the goal) as metrics for efficiency or performance. Safety is evaluated based on \textit{collision rate} and for the gravity of the collision we use \textit{collision speed}, \textit{collision agent speed} and \textit{maximum collision intersection} (the maximal overlap between colliding agents going through one another, a high percentage indicates a greater overlap and a more direct collision). FRP is prominent with classical constraint-based approaches, so we evaluate the advantages against MPC. There is no well-established metric to quantify FRP, we propose to use the instances when the QP-solver fails to find a solution for the given constraints. In this case the last computed braking trajectory is applied resulting potentially in the robot stopping. ProDMP was trained using the \verb!CrowdNavigation!\footnote{\url{link/to/environment}} environment. Further implementations details can be found for all components \verb!MPCCrowdNav!\footnote{\url{link/to/mpc}}, \verb!TrustRegionProjectionLayers!\footnote{\url{link/to/rl_algorithm}}.

This comes at the cost of a reduced success rate because it ignores the crowd and the optimal path relative to it. Combining MPC with a learned RL trajectory results in higher success rate by $\sim$5\%. While a lower TTG means a higher return, ignoring a comfortable distance to the crowd penalises MPC. Furthermore, increasing the distance constraint to optimize this component of the reward would compromise goal-reaching efficiency. As expected, pure RL methods have high return, especially ProDMP while SAC maintains a high collision rate reducing considerably performance. Note that the return is an arbitrary metric, by lowering goal component $C_g$ it is possible to have a higher value for MPC compared to ProDMP, and similarly by increasing the penalty for collisions it is possible to have a higher return for MPC when compared to SAC and ProDMP, in contrast ProDMP MPC remains robust w.r.t. these changes.

\subsection{ORCA Crowd}

By using ORCA \cite{berg_optimal_2011} the crowd prediction is not perfect as the crowd will accelerate and decelerate towards the goal while avoiding collisions. In order to make the task more difficult for the agent, members modeled with ORCA take in consideration only a fraction ($\frac{1}{4}$) of the physical space the agent occupies. This means that the majority of the collision avoidance task has to be undertaken by the agent itself.

Because of the uncertainty in the crowd position, MPC performs quite poorly ($\sim 30\%$ success rate) as it often engages in braking trajectories that lead to crashes given that ORCA does not avoid the entire physical space of the agent. Shortening the horizon (SH) amends that by almost doubling the success rate, however this has a negative effect on safety, results shown in \Cref{plots:orca_mpc}. In order to account for the ORCA crowd that does not avoid crashes completely, we also try to augment the distance (AG) in the MPC constraints. This increases the success rate and return but increases collisions as the agent has less space to navigate in the environment.

Training ProDMP with a short horizon yields much better results in cases where crowd uncertainty is employed, however ignoring crowd uncertainty is still more efficient and arguably safer at the cost of violating passive safety.

\section{DISCUSSION \& CONCLUSION}\label{sec:conclusion}
We introduce a new approach to combine MPC and learning through the use of an episodic-RL algorithm, ProDMP, trained using TRPL, an on-policy RL method. Instead of using a crowd uninformed objective that aims to go directly to the goal through a straight line, we use trajectories parameterized through ProDMP in order to incorporate the infinite-horizon objective learned through RL. In the evaluation we consider different degrees of complexity for the navigation task. A constant moving crowd can be easily predicted and is more adaptable to the MPC framework, however even in this case learning can introduce advantages as it might better avoid deadlock situations in the future. When using ORCA to model the crowd it is necessary to introduce uncertainty in the future position of the crowd. This makes the FRP more eminent. In fact, in this scenario MPC performs really poorly and it can only be improved marginally by relaxing the uncertainty or shortening the horizon. ProDMP MPC introduces a clear improvement in this case.

In GCA it was made clear that without joint-collision avoidance it is difficult to compete with pure learning approaches like \cite{everett_collision_2021}. The main reason for this is the FRP for high-density crowd that results in an explosion of constraint. Ignoring the discrepancies in the evaluating environment which is not the as the one used to train ProDMP MPC (mainly due to the biases introduced from ORCA), it is still clear that there are limitations to forcing passive safety. Relaxing the constraints can lead to more efficient policies, but the main caveat in this scenarios is that the agent should be able to stop quickly enough.

\section{FUTURE WORK}

We see the use of classic control as integral in robotics as it offers the basis for safety while also facilitating an easier and more explainable implementation in the real world. In future work we will try to relax the strong theoretical conditions for MPC by tuning the horizon lengths accordingly, using predictive systems to avoid explosion of uncertainty and comparing to sampling-based MPC approaches. Additionally, it is essential to train our policy with different crowd strategies and noisy sensors to generalize better. Finally, we aim to improve our current approach by incorporating more complex learning architectures like Transformers and diffusion-models.
